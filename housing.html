Coops and runs

Build for dry birds and locked doors

A good coop does not need to be fancy. It needs to be dry, ventilated, easy to clean, and hard for predators to enter. Here is how to size and set one up.

The short version

A good coop does not need to be fancy. It needs to be dry, well ventilated, easy to clean, and hard for predators to get into. If you get those four things right, almost everything else is decoration.

Coop space

Plan about 4 square feet of floor per standard-size hen. Bantams can get by with about half that.

Run space

Plan about 10 square feet of outdoor run per standard-size hen. More room means less pecking and mud.

Roosts

About 8 to 10 inches of roost bar per bird, placed higher than the nest boxes.

Nest boxes

About 1 box for every 3 to 4 hens. They will share, and often all pick the same favorite.

Your city may set its own minimums or maximums. Some cities cap coop size or height, require setbacks from property lines, or ban coops in front yards. Check your local rules before you build or buy.

How big should the coop and run be?

Space is the most common beginner mistake, and it usually goes one way: too small. Prefab coops often advertise capacities like "fits 6 to 8 hens" that only work if the birds are small and spend all day free-ranging. Measure the actual floor area yourself.

The parts of a working setup

Coop

A protected place to sleep and lay, with roosts, nest boxes, ventilation, and a big door for cleaning.

Run

Fenced daytime space with shade, good drainage, and a covered top if hawks or owls are common.

Feed storage

A sealed metal can or heavy bin with a tight lid. Open bags invite mice and rats.

Work path

A layout that lets you fill water, collect eggs, and change bedding without crawling or stepping in mud.

Roosts

Chickens sleep perched, not in nest boxes. A 2x4 laid with the wide side up works well and lets hens cover their toes with their feathers on cold nights. Put roosts higher than the nest boxes so birds do not sleep (and poop) in the boxes. If you have more than one bar, space them so birds are not stacked directly over each other.

Nest boxes

Roughly 12 by 12 inches is a common size for standard hens. Place them lower than the roosts, in a dimmer corner, and add nest pads or clean bedding. Exterior egg doors are a real convenience, especially in winter and with kids.

Doors and access

Look for a people door or a wide clean-out hatch you can actually reach into. Small hatches make cleaning miserable, and a coop that is miserable to clean will not get cleaned.

Ventilation without drafts

Chickens breathe out a surprising amount of moisture, and their droppings add more. Trapped moisture causes ammonia smell, respiratory trouble, and frostbite on combs in cold weather. Ventilation is how you get rid of it.

  • Put vents high on the walls or under the roof line, above the roost height, so damp warm air can leave.
  • Keep strong drafts off the birds at roost level. Air moving above them is good; wind blowing across them on a January night is not.
  • Cover every vent and window with 1/2-inch hardware cloth.
  • Many keepers aim for roughly 1 square foot of vent area per bird. Small prefab coops often have far less, so add vents if you can.
  • If you smell ammonia when you open the door, the coop needs more ventilation, drier bedding, or both.

Do not seal up the coop to keep it warm in winter. Cold-hardy hens handle cold well when they are dry. Damp, closed-up coops are what cause problems.

Predator-proofing basics

Most flock losses come from predators, and most predator losses come from a handful of fixable gaps. The short list:

  • Use 1/2-inch hardware cloth, not chicken wire. Chicken wire keeps chickens in. It does not keep raccoons, dogs, or weasels out.
  • Stop digging. Lay a wire apron about 12 to 24 inches out from the base of the run, or bury the wire about 12 inches down.
  • Use two-step latches. Raccoons can open hooks, twist latches, and simple slide bolts. A carabiner through a latch is a cheap fix.
  • Cover the run. A solid roof or wire top keeps out hawks and climbing predators. Netting stops hawks but not raccoons.
  • Lock up every night. The coop door needs to be closed every single night, either by you or a reliable automatic door you check.

Where to put the coop

  • High and dry. Avoid low spots where water pools. Mud and wet bedding cause most coop smells and foot problems.
  • Shade in summer, shelter in winter. Afternoon shade is valuable. A building, fence, or shrubs on the windward side help in winter.
  • Close enough to the house. You will walk out there every day, in sleet and in the dark. Shorter trips make chores easier to keep up.
  • Near water and power if possible. A heated waterer needs an outdoor-rated, GFCI-protected outlet. Avoid long indoor extension cords; some cities, like Kenosha, specifically prohibit powering coops with extension cords.
  • Inside the legal setbacks. Many cities require coops to be a set distance from neighbors' homes or lot lines, and some ban front-yard coops.
  • Neighbor-friendly. Put the coop away from a neighbor's patio or bedroom window when you can.

Floors, bedding, and cleaning

A coop floor should be solid and cleanable. Wood, sealed plywood, vinyl over plywood, and concrete all work. Dirt floors are hard to keep dry and let rodents dig in, and some cities do not allow them.

Pine shavings are the easy default bedding. They are absorbent and easy to find at farm stores. Avoid cedar shavings, which give off strong aromatic oils, and avoid slick materials like plain newspaper for chicks.

  • Daily or every few days: scoop the droppings under the roost if you use a droppings board.
  • Weekly: remove wet or caked spots, refresh nest boxes, scrub the waterer.
  • A few times a year: full clean-out, scrape surfaces, let it dry, and put in fresh bedding.

Used bedding and manure make good compost for the garden once fully composted. Do not put fresh chicken manure on vegetables you are about to harvest.

Prefab, DIY, or converted shed?

Prefab kit

Fastest. Works for 2 to 4 hens if you measure real floor space, upgrade the latches, and add hardware cloth where the kit uses thin wire.

DIY build

Usually the best value per square foot if you have basic tools. Free plans from extension offices and local builders are easy to find.

Converted shed or playhouse

Often the roomiest option for the money. Add roosts, nest boxes, high vents with hardware cloth, and a pop door.

Used coop

Can be a great deal. Clean and disinfect it well before use, and check for rot and gaps.

Frequently asked questions

How much space does a chicken need in a coop?

Plan about 4 square feet of coop floor per standard-size hen, plus about 10 square feet of run per hen. Bantams need roughly half. Check your city rules too, since some set their own minimums.

How many nest boxes do I need?

About 1 nest box for every 3 to 4 hens. Hens share, so you do not need one per bird. A flock of 4 to 6 hens usually does fine with 2 boxes.

Is chicken wire good enough for a run?

No. Chicken wire keeps chickens in but raccoons, dogs, and weasels can tear or reach through it. Use 1/2-inch hardware cloth on the run, windows, and vents, and add an apron or buried edge to stop digging.

Do I need to heat my chicken coop in winter?

Usually not. Cold-hardy breeds handle cold well when the coop is dry, ventilated, and free of drafts at roost level. Heat lamps cause coop fires. Focus on keeping water from freezing instead.

Can chickens free-range instead of having a run?

Some cities require chickens to stay in a coop or enclosed run at all times. Where free-ranging is allowed, supervised time in a fenced yard is safest. Free-ranging birds face more predator risk and may wander into neighbors' yards.

How often should I clean the coop?

Spot-clean wet bedding and nest boxes weekly, scrub waterers weekly, and do a full clean-out a few times a year. If you smell ammonia, clean sooner and improve ventilation.
