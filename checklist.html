First-Year Chicken Starter Checklist

Everything to check, build, buy, and watch during your first year with backyard hens. Print it, stick it on the fridge, and check things off as you go.

1. Check the rules before you spend money

  • Look up your city, village, or town ordinance (start with our local law finder). If you live outside city limits, check county zoning too.
  • Write down the flock limit, rooster rule, coop setbacks from property lines and neighbors, and any front-yard ban.
  • Find out if you need a permit or license, how much it costs, when it renews, and whether neighbors must be notified.
  • Check HOA rules, deed restrictions, or your lease. A landlord may need to sign the application.
  • In some states, such as Wisconsin, you must register your property with the state livestock premises program before a city will issue a chicken license.

2. Plan a flock you can actually care for

  • Pick a number within your local limit. Three to six hens is a practical first flock for most families.
  • Choose calm, hardy breeds that fit your winters and summers (try the breed quiz).
  • Decide on day-old chicks (cheaper birds, more setup and about 6 to 8 weeks indoors) or started pullets (more per bird, less work, eggs sooner).
  • Buy from a clean source: an NPIP-participating hatchery, a feed store that orders from one, or a local breeder who lets you see the birds.
  • Skip the rooster unless your rules allow one and your neighbors are fine with crowing.

3. Coop and run basics

  • Coop floor space: plan about 4 square feet per standard-size hen.
  • Run space: plan about 10 square feet per standard-size hen. More is better.
  • Roost bar: about 8 to 10 inches of roost per bird, higher than the nest boxes.
  • Nest boxes: about 1 box for every 3 to 4 hens, in a darker, quiet spot.
  • Ventilation high on the walls, above the birds' heads, so moist air can escape without drafts on roosting hens.
  • Use 1/2-inch hardware cloth (not chicken wire) on windows, vents, and the run.
  • Add a buried or flat "apron" of wire around the run edge to stop digging.
  • Use two-step latches. Raccoons can open simple hooks and slide bolts.
  • Put the coop on high, dry ground and plan a path for winter chores.

4. Supplies to have before birds come home

  • Feeder and waterer sized so you are not refilling every day.
  • Heated waterer or heated base if your area freezes in winter (plus a safe outdoor-rated outlet).
  • Feed for the bird's age: chick starter, then grower, then layer feed once laying starts.
  • Grit if birds eat anything besides complete feed, and oyster shell in a separate dish for laying hens.
  • Bedding such as pine shavings, and nest box liners or pads.
  • A sealed metal or heavy plastic bin for feed storage.
  • Basic first-aid kit: gloves, saline, wound spray made for poultry, a dog crate or box for isolating a sick bird.
  • Dedicated chore boots or boot covers for biosecurity.
  • A headlamp for evening lock-up checks.

5. If you are starting with chicks

  • Brooder box with draft-free sides, set up and warmed a day before chicks arrive.
  • Heat source with a thermometer at chick level. Start around 95°F the first week and lower about 5°F each week until they are feathered or it matches room temperature.
  • Watch the chicks: huddled under heat means too cold; spread far from heat and panting means too hot.
  • Chick waterer with a shallow lip, chick feeder, and chick starter feed.
  • Paper towels over bedding for the first few days so chicks learn what food is.
  • Check for pasty butt (droppings stuck to the vent) daily during the first week.
  • Plan to move chicks to the coop once fully feathered, usually around 6 to 8 weeks, depending on weather.

6. The first week with your flock

  • Keep new birds in the coop and run (not free-ranging) for the first several days so they learn where home is.
  • Check feed and water twice a day until you know how fast they use it.
  • Lock the coop every night, or confirm the automatic door actually closed.
  • Watch each bird for a few minutes a day: eating, drinking, moving normally, clean vent, bright eyes.
  • Keep new birds separate from any existing flock for about 30 days before mixing.
  • Wash hands after handling birds, eggs, or equipment, and keep young children supervised.

7. Through the first year

  • Weekly: scrub waterers, remove wet bedding, check latches, wire, and for digging.
  • Monthly: look for mites and lice under feathers around the vent, top off bedding, check feed for mold.
  • Spring: expect more eggs as days get longer. Watch for broody hens.
  • Summer: shade, extra water, and airflow. Hot weather is harder on chickens than cold.
  • Fall: expect molting and fewer eggs. Hens often slow down or stop as days shorten.
  • Winter: keep water thawed, coop dry and ventilated, and watch combs for frostbite.

8. Know who to call before you need them

  • A veterinarian near you who sees chickens (call before an emergency to ask).
  • Your county extension office for local poultry help.
  • Your state veterinarian or the USDA at 1-866-536-7593 to report sudden deaths or signs of bird flu.
  • Your city or county office that handles chicken permits.

This checklist is general guidance for beginners. Local rules vary a lot, so always confirm with your city or county before buying birds or building a coop. Ask a veterinarian about any sick bird.
