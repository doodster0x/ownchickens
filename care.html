Make chicken chores boring

The best routine is quick, repeatable, and easy to notice when something is off. Most days, a small flock takes about as much time as walking a dog: a few minutes in the morning and a few in the evening. Set up the coop so the daily loop is easy, and you will stick with it.

Daily loop

Morning

  • Open the coop, or confirm the automatic door opened.
  • Check that the waterer is full, clean, and not frozen.
  • Check the feeder level.
  • Count birds and take a quick look: everyone up, moving, and eating?

Afternoon or evening

  • Collect eggs at least once a day, more often in very hot or freezing weather.
  • Close and lock the coop after everyone is on the roost, or verify the automatic door closed with all birds inside.
  • Bring in feeders overnight if rodents are a problem.

Weekly reset

Bedding

Remove wet or heavily soiled spots, refresh nest boxes, and add fresh shavings as needed.

Waterers

Scrub out slime and algae before it turns into a health problem.

Run check

Look for digging, loose wire, weak latches, sagging netting, and sharp edges.

Bird check

Watch for limping, weight loss, mites, dirty vents, pale combs, or a bird off by herself.

Monthly and seasonal jobs

  • Monthly: pick up a hen or two and look under the feathers near the vent for mites and lice. Check feet for swelling or scabs. Top off grit and oyster shell.
  • Spring: laying picks up as days get longer. Expect a broody hen or two. Deep-clean the coop on a dry day.
  • Summer: add shade and extra water, and check water twice a day in heat waves. See keeping chickens cool.
  • Fall: expect molting and fewer eggs. Fix the roof and vents before winter. See molting: what to expect.
  • Winter: keep water thawed, keep bedding dry, and watch combs for frostbite. See your first winter with chickens.

Eggs: what to expect

Egg production surprises a lot of first-time keepers, mostly because it changes so much with age and season.

  • First eggs: most hens start laying somewhere around 18 to 24 weeks old, depending on breed and time of year. Pullets that mature in fall may wait until spring.
  • Peak years: hens lay best in their first two years or so. Production tapers off each year after that, though many hens keep laying some eggs for years.
  • Daylight matters: laying depends on day length. Hens generally need around 14 hours of light for steady laying, so production often drops in fall and winter.
  • Molting: most hens take a break each fall to replace feathers. Laying usually slows or stops for several weeks.
  • A good layer is not one egg a day forever. Even strong layers skip days. Plan on fewer eggs than the "eggs per year" numbers you see advertised.

Some keepers add a light on a timer in winter to keep eggs coming. It works, but many prefer to let hens rest naturally. If you add light, use a safe, secured fixture, add it in the morning rather than the evening, and never use a heat lamp as a light source.

Handling eggs safely

  • Collect eggs at least daily and refrigerate them.
  • Toss cracked eggs or ones that sat out in serious heat.
  • If eggs are dirty, brush off dry dirt first. If you wash them, use water warmer than the egg and do not soak them. Refrigerate washed eggs.
  • Clean nest boxes mean cleaner eggs, which means less washing.
  • Wash your hands after collecting eggs or handling birds. The CDC backyard poultry page has simple tips for families.

Check your local rules before selling or giving away eggs. Many cities limit backyard chickens to personal use, and some prohibit selling eggs from the property.

Know your normal

Every flock has patterns. Pay attention to who eats first, who hangs back, how fast the feeder empties, and what normal droppings look like. Small changes are easier to catch when you know the baseline. A hen that is fluffed up, hunched, or standing alone while others forage needs a closer look. See the health guide for what to watch and when to call a vet.

Going away for a few days

  • A large feeder and waterer can cover a day or two, but someone should still check on the birds daily.
  • An automatic door handles lock-up, but cannot tell you if water froze or a bird is sick.
  • Leave your chicken sitter a short written checklist: feed, water, eggs, lock-up, and who to call.
  • Trade egg-sitting with another chicken keeper. Ask them to wear clean boots and not bring anything from their own coop.

Pecking order and adding new hens

Chickens sort out a social ranking, the pecking order. Some chasing and pecking is normal, especially when birds are new or the flock changes. Blood, a hen being kept from food and water, or constant bullying is not normal and needs a fix.

  • Quarantine new birds for about 30 days first, then let them see the flock through a fence for a week or so before mixing.
  • Add at least two new birds at a time so no single newcomer takes all the attention.
  • Put out an extra feeder and waterer during introductions.
  • Add perches, boxes, or a pallet leaned on the wall so lower-ranked birds can get out of sight.
  • Wait until young birds are close to adult size before mixing them with grown hens.

Planning for older hens

Backyard hens often live several years past their best laying years. Decide early what you will do with older hens: keep them as pets and garden helpers, rehome them, or other options allowed where you live. Note that some cities prohibit on-site slaughter, and some do not let you drop chickens at animal control. Having a plan up front avoids hard decisions later.

Frequently asked questions

How much time do backyard chickens take each day?

For a small flock with a good setup, usually a few minutes in the morning and a few in the evening, plus a weekly clean-up of about half an hour. Bigger chores like a full coop clean-out happen a few times a year.

When will my hens start laying eggs?

Most hens start laying around 18 to 24 weeks old, depending on breed and season. Birds that mature in late fall may not start until days get longer in spring.

Why did my chickens stop laying?

Common reasons are shorter fall and winter days, molting, age, stress, heat, a broody hen, poor nutrition, or illness. Seasonal slowdowns are normal; a sudden stop with other signs of illness is worth a closer look.

How many eggs will I get from my flock?

It varies a lot with breed, age, and season. Young hens of strong laying breeds lay most days in spring and summer and much less in fall and winter. Plan conservatively rather than relying on advertised eggs-per-year numbers.

Do I need to wash eggs from backyard chickens?

Clean eggs can be stored without washing. If you wash eggs, use water warmer than the egg, do not soak them, and refrigerate them afterward. Keeping nest boxes clean reduces the need to wash.

Who will take care of my chickens when I travel?

A neighbor, friend, or pet sitter can check feed, water, eggs, and lock-up daily. Leave written instructions and a contact for a vet. Ask them not to bring anything from other flocks.
