Buying guide

Heated waterers and coop heaters

In cold climates, the piece of winter equipment you truly need is usually a heated waterer, not a coop heater. Here is how to choose safely.

Start here: most flocks do not need a coop heater

Healthy, cold-hardy hens handle winter well when the coop is dry, ventilated, and free of drafts at roost level. They fluff their feathers and huddle on the roost. Problems in cold weather usually come from moisture (frostbite, respiratory trouble) and frozen water, not from cold air itself.

Heat lamps are a common cause of coop fires. Bedding, dust, and feathers burn easily, and lamps can fall or be knocked down. We do not recommend heat lamps in adult coops.

What you probably do need: a heated waterer

If your water freezes, hens cannot drink, and they stop laying or worse. Two common options:

  • Heated waterer: a waterer with a built-in heating element.
  • Heated base: a heated plate you set a metal waterer on. Check which waterers it is meant for.

What to look for

  • Made for poultry or outdoor use, with a thermostat so it only heats near freezing.
  • A safety certification mark from a recognized testing lab on the product or packaging.
  • A cord long enough to reach your outlet without a long extension cord.
  • A design you can scrub easily.

Electrical safety basics

  • Plug into a GFCI-protected outdoor outlet. A permanent outdoor circuit is safest; ask an electrician if you are unsure.
  • If you must use an extension cord, use a heavy-duty outdoor-rated cord, keep connections dry, and do not run it through doors. Some cities prohibit powering coops with extension cords and require an electrical permit for coop power.
  • Keep cords away from chickens, which peck at them, and out of bedding.
  • Do not build homemade heaters from bulbs, heat tape, or cookie tins.
  • Check plugs and cords for wear every fall.

If you do add coop heat

Some keepers add gentle heat for a sick bird, very small bantams, or extreme cold snaps. If you do:

  • Use a flat radiant panel heater designed for coops or animal housing, mounted securely per the instructions, not a heat lamp.
  • Keep it away from bedding and roosts, and keep the coop ventilated.
  • Aim to take the edge off, not make the coop warm. Birds that live in a heated coop handle power outages poorly.

Frequently asked questions

Do chickens need a heater in the winter?

Most healthy, cold-hardy hens do not. A dry, well-ventilated coop without drafts at roost level matters more. Keep water from freezing instead.

Are heat lamps safe in a chicken coop?

Heat lamps are a common cause of coop fires. We do not recommend them in adult coops. If you need heat, use a flat radiant panel made for animal housing, mounted securely.

What is the best way to keep chicken water from freezing?

A thermostatically controlled heated waterer or heated base made for poultry or outdoor use, plugged into a GFCI-protected outdoor outlet.
