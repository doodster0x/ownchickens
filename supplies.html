Pick the setup that matches your budget

There are two sane ways to build your first chicken setup: buy it fast, or spend more time sourcing and building. The Convenience List is the easy Amazon route for people who want fewer decisions and faster setup. The Budget List mixes local sourcing with handy building for people who have more time than cash. 01 / Housing

Prefab coop or coop/run combo

Start with a simple backyard coop sized for 2 to 4 hens. For convenience, look for one with built in nesting boxes that have exterior egg access, so you can collect eggs without going inside.

02 / Automation

Automatic coop door

A battery powered door is one of the biggest time savers you can buy. Hens naturally head inside before dark, and the door closes behind them automatically. Most only need fresh batteries about every 6 months.

Search Amazon
03 / Food

Feeder

A simple gravity feeder you only have to fill every other week, maybe longer. The more it holds, the less often you touch it. Just make sure it fits inside your coop.

Search Amazon
04 / Food

Chicken food

Buy the bigger bags. Higher cost up front, but it lasts a lot longer and brings your cost per pound down. Feed quality has a direct effect on egg quality, so this is not the place to cut corners.

Search Amazon
05 / Water

Easy-clean waterer

Clean water is essential. The larger the waterer, the less often you refill it. A bigger one means one less thing to think about during the week.

Search Amazon
06 / Cold weather

Heated water base or heated waterer

Live in a warm climate? Skip this. If your area freezes in winter, it is essential for keeping water from icing over. Plug it into a GFCI-protected outdoor outlet. See the winter heat guide for safety tips.

Search Amazon
07 / Bedding

Coop bedding

Pine shavings are a great go to. They keep the inside of the coop dry and comfortable for the hens. Add a little every 2 to 4 weeks, and do a full replacement every 5 to 6 months or whenever it builds up too much.

08 / Laying support

Nest box pads or liners

Nesting pads are optional, but many keepers find they help keep eggs cleaner and are easy to shake out or wash.

The Budget List

Local sourcing and handy building

This list is for people who have more time than cash. It covers what to look for used, what to build, material lists for a small coop and run, and where not to cut corners.

We do not list prices here because local and used prices vary too much. For rough planning ranges, use the cost calculator, and always check local prices.

Look for these used first

Used coops

Often listed on Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, and local buy-nothing groups when people move or downsize. Check for rot, gaps, and a solid floor, then clean and disinfect before use.

Sheds and playhouses

A small shed or kids' playhouse can become a roomy coop. Add roosts, nest boxes, a pop door, and high vents covered with hardware cloth.

Dog kennels

Chain-link kennel panels make a quick, sturdy run frame. Wrap the lower walls and any gaps with 1/2-inch hardware cloth, add a roof, and add a dig apron.

Building salvage

Habitat for Humanity ReStores and salvage yards often carry plywood, doors, windows, hinges, and roofing at a discount.

Pallets

Fine for compost bins and some framing. Only use pallets stamped "HT" (heat treated). Avoid pallets stamped "MB" (methyl bromide) and any that look stained or oily.

Free bedding and extras

Shredded dry leaves and straw from local farms work as bedding in a pinch. Ask landscapers or neighbors.

Material list: small coop for about 4 hens

A simple 4 by 4 foot raised coop gives about 16 square feet of floor, enough for about 4 standard hens. Free plans from extension offices and local builders are easy to find; adjust these quantities to the plan you pick.

Frame and floor
  • 2x4 lumber for the frame and legs (pressure-treated only for parts touching the ground)
  • Exterior plywood for the floor
  • Optional: vinyl flooring scrap or paint for an easy-clean floor
Walls and roof
  • Exterior plywood, siding, or salvaged panels for walls
  • Roofing: metal panels or shingles over plywood
  • Drip edge or trim so rain runs off, not in
Inside the coop
  • A 2x4 roost bar, wide side up, about 8 to 10 inches per hen
  • One or two nest boxes (a plywood box, milk crate, or 5-gallon bucket on its side)
  • A droppings board under the roost (optional, makes cleaning easier)
Openings and hardware
  • 1/2-inch hardware cloth for vents and windows
  • Hinges for a people door or large clean-out door
  • A small pop door for the hens
  • Two-step latches, plus carabiners for extra security
  • Exterior screws and fender washers
Finish
  • Exterior paint or sealant, fully dried and aired out before birds move in
  • Caulk for gaps where drafts or rain get in

Material list: run for about 4 hens

A 4 by 10 foot run gives about 40 square feet, or about 10 square feet per hen.

Frame
  • 2x4 lumber, metal fence posts, or kennel panels
  • A simple framed door with a two-step latch
Wire
  • 1/2-inch hardware cloth for the walls (at least the lower part; all of it if raccoons are common)
  • Extra hardware cloth for a 12 to 24 inch apron around the base
  • Landscape staples or pavers to pin the apron down
Roof
  • Metal or polycarbonate roofing panels, or a hardware cloth top
  • A tarp or shade cloth for summer shade if the roof is wire
Floor
  • Sand, wood chips, or leaves to keep the run from turning to mud
  • Gravel or pavers by the door for drainage

DIY feeders and waterers

  • Bucket feeder: a food-safe bucket with a lid and PVC elbow feed ports. Holds a lot and keeps feed dry.
  • Bucket waterer: a food-safe bucket with poultry nipples or horizontal cups. Stays cleaner than an open pan.
  • Grit and oyster shell: two small dishes or a divided feeder mounted on the wall.
  • Feed storage: a galvanized trash can with a tight lid.

Do not build your own heated waterer from bulbs, heat tape, or other homemade electrical setups. Coop fires are a real risk. If your water freezes, buy a heated waterer or base listed for outdoor or poultry use and plug it into a GFCI-protected outdoor outlet.

Where cheap is fine, and where it is not

Fine to go cheap or used

Feeders, nest boxes, feed bins, bedding storage, the coop exterior and paint color, run flooring, and decorations.

Do not cheap out on

Hardware cloth (not chicken wire), latches, a solid roof, a dry floor, complete feed from a reputable mill, and any heated waterer or electrical device.

The tradeoff: time

A budget build saves cash but costs time. Plan for sourcing (watching listings and picking up materials), a few weekends of building, and fixing things you find after the first rain or the first raccoon visit. If time is the tighter resource, the Convenience List above is the faster path. Many people mix the two: a used coop plus new hardware cloth, latches, and a store-bought heated waterer.
